# How to answer a legal question

The reader is not a lawyer, is paying attention, and wants to **check the
reasoning and argue with it**. Write for that reader.

## The structure

Lay the evidence out **before** the argument. Leading with the conclusion asks
the reader to trust it and audit backwards; leading with the sources lets them
follow the reasoning as it is built, and challenge it.

### 1. Question and assumptions
Restate the question, and name the facts the answer depends on. State every
assumption — a wrong assumption is the most likely reason the answer is wrong.

### 2. The sources that govern this
Before any analysis. List every instrument that bears on the question, named in
full, and **paste the operative text**. This section is the evidence; the reader
should be able to read it and start forming their own view.

For each provision:

> **Article 6(1)(f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (the General Data Protection
> Regulation, "GDPR")** — text from CELEX 02016R0679-20160504
> [link](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02016R0679-20160504)
>
> "processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued
> by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are
> overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data
> subject…"
>
> In plain terms: [one or two sentences].

Czech provisions are quoted in Czech, with the English gloss after.

### 3. How they apply
The reasoning. Tie it to the **specific words** in the text quoted above — which
phrase does the work, and why. This is the part that is genuinely yours rather
than the source's, so make the join visible.

### 4. Conclusion
The answer. If there isn't a clear one, say so plainly and state **what the
answer depends on** — the fact, the untested question, or the missing source.
An honest "this turns on X, which no court has decided" is a real conclusion.

### 5. Next steps, in priority order
Concrete actions, most important first. Mark plainly anything that needs a
qualified Czech advocate rather than more research. This section turns a report
into candidate follow-up work, which is much of its value.

### 6. Sources consulted and searches run
Every source, with links. The **Czech search terms actually used** — so a bad
term is visible and correctable. Coverage limits of the indexes. And where
nothing was found, say "these searches found nothing", never "there is none".

## Rules that are not optional

**Every cited provision carries its text.** If a provision does any work in the
reasoning, it gets: the source document named in full on first mention, the text
quoted, a link, and the version date. **If it is not worth quoting, it is not
worth citing** — drop it, or say explicitly that you did not check it.

Half-substantiating is worse than not substantiating at all: it teaches the
reader that everything cited was checked, while some of it silently was not.
Before delivering, sweep the draft for every provision reference and confirm each
one has its text.

**Expand every abbreviation on first use.** In full, with the short form in
brackets after: "data protection impact assessment (DPIA)". This applies to
legal, technical and institutional abbreviations alike. You cannot catch these
by rereading your own writing — check deliberately.

**Never judge the reader's time.** No "worth ten minutes", no "quick check", no
deciding for them whether something is worth doing. Instead: say the item
exists, **qualify how critical it is to the decision** —

- *could change the answer*
- *refines the answer but does not change it*
- *background only*

— and give a link they can click, whether that is a URL or a local file path.

**Quote the source language.** Never translate a Czech provision and present the
translation as the text. Quote the Czech, gloss it in English.

**Version everything.** "As in force from 2025-07-01" is part of the citation,
not decoration. Use `legal_cite` with `as_of` where the facts are historical.

## Tone and length

The project-wide writing rules in `CLAUDE.md` apply here in full: no em-dashes or
en-dashes, no three-item lists by default, no "it is not X, it is Y" phrasing, no
preamble or closing pleasantries.

Plain language, short sentences. Keep the Czech term in brackets on first use
where it is the term of art — *employee work (zaměstnanecké dílo)* — because the
Czech word is what a search or a lawyer will recognise.

No hedging padding. If something is clear, say so. If it is not, say what is
missing. Sounding confident about an uncertain point is the one thing that is
not acceptable.

Match the length to the question. A factual lookup gets a short answer with a
quoted provision. A question that could cost money gets the full structure.
